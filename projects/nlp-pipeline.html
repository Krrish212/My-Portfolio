An End-to-End NLP Sentiment Pipeline, Built Incrementally

Classifying 4,000 IMDb reviews by hand-building every stage — cleaning, tokenisation, vectorisation, weighting, and a Naive Bayes classifier written from scratch — and changing exactly one thing at a time.

Text is noisy. Most of the work is deciding what to throw away.

Binary sentiment classification over a balanced subset of the Large Movie Review Dataset: 2,000 positive and 2,000 negative reviews, labelled from star ratings (≤4 negative, ≥7 positive) with neutral reviews excluded.

The interesting part isn't the accuracy number. It's that every design decision had to be earned. I split the data 70/15/15 with stratified sampling, then built the pipeline one component at a time — each change evaluated on the development set alone, kept only if it helped, and the test set opened exactly once at the end.

That discipline is what makes the results readable. When accuracy moves, I can name the single cause.

The winning configuration turned out to be the least clever one available: lowercased text with stop words removed, lexical unigrams through trigrams, no frequency cutoff, raw counts, and Multinomial Naive Bayes. Every weighting scheme I tried made it worse.

One review of fourteen words expands into dozens of features. Across 2,800 training documents this produced a 613,482-dimensional space in which almost every cell is zero — which is precisely why the matrix is stored sparsely and why a model with strong independence assumptions holds up so well.

04Weighting

Three weighting schemes. None of them beat raw counts.

A document–term matrix treats each review as a point in vocabulary-space, one axis per feature. That representation is honest but skewed: Zipf's law means a handful of terms dominate every document, so the standard move is to reweight the axes before classifying. I implemented three schemes and none of them helped.

L2 normalisation rescales each row to unit length so long reviews don't shout over short ones. TF–IDF multiplies term frequency by an inverse document frequency term, suppressing anything that appears everywhere. PPMI is the one I found most interesting — instead of measuring rarity, it measures association between a feature and a class.

PMI(w, y) = log[ P(w, y) / (P(w) · P(y)) ]
PPMI(w, y) = max( PMI(w, y), 0 )
weight(w) = max over classes y

Zero means the word and the class are independent; positive means the word shows up with that class more than chance predicts. Clipping negatives keeps only the informative direction. Weights are learned on the training split alone and then applied as a column scaling to dev and test — computing them on the full corpus would leak the labels.

Why TF–IDF backfired here

IDF assumes that a term appearing in most documents is uninformative. In sentiment that assumption breaks: bad, boring and brilliant are common and decisive. Down-weighting by document frequency strips the signal out. Spread across a 613k-dimensional n-gram space, the surviving weights get small enough that genuinely discriminative phrases stop carrying the decision.

The frequency-threshold sweep is the clearest trade-off in the set: raising the cutoff from 1 to 2 deletes 92% of the vocabulary and costs a full point of accuracy. Rare trigrams are sparse, but they aren't noise — a phrase seen once in training is still evidence. With a probabilistic model and Laplace smoothing absorbing the tail, there was no reason to prune.

06Classifier

Naive Bayes, derived from Bayes' theorem and rebuilt.

I wrote the classifier from scratch rather than calling it, because the assumptions are the whole story — and because matching a reference implementation exactly is the only real proof you understood them.

  1. P(c | x) = P(c) · P(x | c) / P(x)

    Bayes' theorem. The posterior we want, in terms of a prior we can count and a likelihood we can model.

  2. P(c | x) ∝ P(c) · P(x | c)

    The evidence P(x) is identical across classes, so it can't change the argmax. Drop it.

  3. P(x | c) = ∏ᵢ P(xᵢ | c)

    The naive assumption: features are conditionally independent given the class. False for language — but the errors it introduces are largely symmetric across classes, so the ranking survives.

  4. θ_c,i = (N(wᵢ,c) + α) / (Σⱼ N(wⱼ,c) + αV)

    Multinomial likelihood with Laplace smoothing. Without α, one unseen feature drives the entire product to zero. Tuning on dev gave α = 1.0.

  5. ĉ = argmax [ log P(c) + Σᵢ xᵢ · log P(wᵢ|c) ]

    Move to log space. Multiplying 613,482 small probabilities underflows float64; summing logs turns the whole scoring step into one sparse matrix–matrix product.

# Fit: class priors + smoothed per-class feature log-probs
for ci, c in enumerate(self.classes_):
    X_c        = X[y == c]
    N_c[ci]    = (y == c).sum()
    N_w_c[ci]  = np.asarray(X_c.sum(axis=0)).ravel()

self.class_log_prior_ = np.log(N_c / N_c.sum())

smoothed = N_w_c + alpha
N_dot_c  = smoothed.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
self.feature_log_prob_ = np.log(smoothed / N_dot_c)

# Predict: the entire argmax is one sparse product
jll = X @ self.feature_log_prob_.T
jll = np.asarray(jll) + self.class_log_prior_
return self.classes_[jll.argmax(axis=1)]

Verification

The from-scratch model and sklearn.naive_bayes.MultinomialNB returned identical test accuracy to four decimal places on the frozen pipeline — 0.8417 against 0.8417. Identical, not merely close, which is the result you should expect when the maths lines up and the smoothing constant matches.

07Results

The simplest model won. The most flexible one collapsed.

The feature pipeline was frozen on development-set evidence alone, then evaluated once on the held-out test split.

The decision tree's 14-point collapse is the informative failure. Axis-aligned splits need individual features that partition the data, but in a 613,482-dimensional space where each feature fires in a handful of documents, no single split carries much information — the tree memorises the training set and generalises badly.

Naive Bayes goes the other way. It accumulates weak evidence additively across every feature present, which is exactly the right inductive bias when the signal is spread thin across a very wide, very sparse matrix.

08Takeaway

Every technique that discarded information cost accuracy.

Stemming, PoS abstraction, frequency pruning, IDF down-weighting — each one lost ground. Everything that preserved lexical detail gained it.

That isn't a universal law; it's what 4,000 documents and a bag-of-words model will tell you. But arriving at it through controlled, single-variable experiments rather than assumption is the part that transfers. The honest conclusion of this project is that the sophisticated options lost, and I have the ablation table to prove it.

The ceiling here is the representation, not the classifier. Bag-of-words discards order beyond a three-token window and treats brilliant and superb as unrelated axes. Contextual embeddings dissolve both limits, which is the direction I've taken the work since — into dense retrieval, vector similarity and RAG systems, where the same questions about what a good representation is come back in a different form.
